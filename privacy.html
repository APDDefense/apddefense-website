Privacy Policy

One policy, covering three Android apps: PulseLink, CommandLink and TerraLink.

Effective date: September 24, 2026  ·  Version 1.1

In short

These three Android apps are companions to an APD Defense encrypted mesh radio. They are built to work with no cell service and no internet.

  • We do not collect your data. APD Defense operates no server for these apps. Nothing you enter or record is sent to us. We cannot see it.
  • There are no ads, no analytics, no trackers and no crash reporting services. We checked the code, not just our intentions: there is no advertising library, no analytics library, no crash reporting library and no Google Play Services dependency in any of the three apps.
  • You need no account. There is nothing to sign up for and no login.
  • Your records stay on your phone, in each app's private storage, which other apps on the phone cannot read.
  • What leaves the phone goes over your radio, to the other radios on your own net, encrypted and authenticated by the radio hardware — and only when you send it.
  • These apps are prototypes. They are not medical devices, they are not FDA cleared, and they are not certified or approved for any purpose.

The rest of this policy says exactly what each app holds, where it holds it, and what can leave.

Health information in PulseLink

This is the most sensitive information any of the three apps handles, so it gets its own section.

What PulseLink holds. Casualty records you create: a name and call sign from the roster you typed, the triage category shown for that person, the MARCH checklist and tourniquet times you recorded, what treatment you noted, the disposition you set, the last position reported for them, and any readings that arrived from their radio.

Where it lives. On your phone, in PulseLink's private storage. One file per mission. Capped at 10 missions and 30 days and pruned automatically. No other app on the phone can read it. PulseLink's records are excluded from Android cloud backup.

Where it can go — exactly two routes, both of them yours.

  1. A 9-line MEDEVAC request you write and send. You fill it in, you review it against what the app read back to you, and you press send. It goes out over the radio as a broadcast to every radio on your net; a leader running CommandLink sees it as a 9-line. The radio encrypts and authenticates it with the network key before it goes on the air.
  2. A roster push you choose to send, so a leader's app can show who is carrying which radio. This sends call signs only unless you explicitly ask for names each time — the app defaults to call signs and requires you to opt in to names on every push.

There is no third route, and this is structural rather than a promise. PulseLink contains no network code at all: no socket, no web client, no connection of any kind is created anywhere in it. The part of the shared code that could relay a casualty over a network is wired, in PulseLink, to an implementation that does nothing. The only thing in PulseLink's whole dependency chain that can open a network connection is the map tile downloader, which only fetches map images and has no access to the casualty board, the roster or a 9-line.

Nothing goes to APD Defense. We have no server for these apps and no way to receive a casualty record.

Vital signs — read this before you expect any

Important

The APD radios carry no vital-sign sensors. There is no pulse sensor, no oxygen sensor and no thermometer on the radio hardware. A live radio therefore reports no vitals at all, and the app shows dashes where a reading would go.

The apps are built so they never invent a value. “No reading” is carried as a specific value that means “nothing was reported”, kept deliberately distinct from a real measurement of zero, and shown on screen as dashes. A radio can report a motion state — moving, still, or immobile long enough that the radio itself flags it — and that is the only body-related reading the fielded hardware sends.

Made-up readings appear on screen in exactly one situation: Training Mode, which paints every screen magenta, marks every card SIM, and is never saved and never archived.

Not a medical device

Not a medical device

PulseLink is a prototype. It is decision support and a record-keeping aid for a trained medic. It does not diagnose, monitor, treat or prevent anything, and nothing it shows is a clinical finding.

  • It is not a medical device, has no FDA clearance or approval, and has not been clinically validated.
  • It must not be relied on for medical care, for patient monitoring, or for any decision on which someone's safety depends.
  • The medic decides. The app shows what arrived and what the medic entered, and the assessment of the person on the ground overrides it in every case.
  • The app itself says so on screen: DECISION SUPPORT ONLY — NOT A MEDICAL DEVICE.

CommandLink and TerraLink are prototypes on the same terms, and neither is a medical device either.

Location

What is collected. Your phone's own position, from the Android platform location service, using the GPS provider and the network provider when one is available. The apps do not use Google Play Services' fused location provider and do not depend on Play Services at all.

When. Only while a screen that needs it is open, and only after you grant the location permission. Location is reference-counted: the Map screen and the 9-line form each turn it on while they are showing and turn it off when they close, so the GPS is not left running in your pocket. No app has background location permission, and none of them tracks you when you are not using them.

Analytics, advertising and third-party code

There are none. No advertising SDK, no analytics SDK, no crash-reporting service, no attribution or marketing SDK, no Google Play Services, no Firebase, no push service, no A/B testing service, no remote configuration and no license check. Nothing in these apps contacts an APD Defense server, because there is no APD Defense server for them to contact.

The third-party code the apps do include is open-source library code that runs entirely on your phone and sends nothing anywhere:

  • AndroidX and Jetpack Compose (Google) — the user interface and camera plumbing.
  • Kotlin coroutines (JetBrains) — background work.
  • ZXing core — decoding and drawing QR codes, on the device.
  • usb-serial-for-android — talking to the radio over the USB cable.

The one thing that reaches the internet

If, and only if, you switch on Map Downloads and choose an area, the app fetches map tiles from the United States Geological Survey's The National Map service (basemap.nationalmap.gov). What it sends is the coordinates of the tiles you asked for and a user-agent string that identifies the app and nothing about you; USGS will see your IP address, as any web server does. The app only fetches — it uploads nothing, and the downloader has no access to your records, your roster or your messages. A short list of map hosts is hard-coded; the app cannot be pointed at an arbitrary server. USGS map data is public domain, and the license and credit line are written into each map file.

No map host is contacted while you are on a mission. Maps are downloaded before you go.

Working offline

These apps are offline-capable by design, not offline-restricted. Everything they do works with no cell service, no Wi-Fi and no internet at all, because the radio net is the point. Downloading a map is the one networked feature, and with it off or unreachable every app is fully functional.

Security

  • Over the air: the radio, not the phone, is the encryption endpoint. Traffic on the mesh is encrypted and authenticated by the radio hardware with a network key. PulseLink holds no keys at all. CommandLink and TerraLink can hold keys, under the phone's hardware key store, as described above.
  • On the phone: app-private storage, under the phone's own encryption. Keys and the admin PIN get the extra hardware-backed layer described above. Use a lock screen — key material is bound to it.
  • Backups: all three apps are excluded from Android cloud backup. CommandLink and TerraLink additionally exclude themselves from device-to-device transfer. Being precise, because this policy should not overclaim: PulseLink currently disables cloud backup but does not carry the separate device-transfer exclusion rules that the other two do. We are reviewing that.
  • The cable is not encrypted. The USB link between phone and radio carries plain data. Keep the radio with you. When CommandLink or TerraLink loads a key into a radio that already has an owner, the key crosses the cable wrapped under that radio's owner secret; the first key loaded into a radio with no owner crosses it unwrapped.
  • The radio's debug port is open on current prototype hardware, so someone with physical possession of a radio and the right tools can read its network key. Treat a lost radio as a lost key: the fleet should be re-keyed. Treat this hardware as unclassified.
  • The app and the radio update differently. An app installed from Google Play may be updated by Google Play according to your phone's own update settings; the app cannot override them. If you want no automatic app updates on a phone (for example a mission phone), turn off auto-update for it in Google Play. Radio firmware never updates without your explicit consent: CommandLink shows you what will happen and how long it will take, and asks you to choose Update Now or Later — Later is the default, and an update can be declined or postponed freely.

Children

These apps are not directed at children and are not designed or marketed for anyone under 13. They are professional tools for radio operators, team leaders and medics, and they need APD Defense radio hardware to do anything real. We do not knowingly collect information from children — we do not collect information from anyone. If you believe a child has used one of these apps in a way you are concerned about, email us and we will help you clear the phone.
